Product plan · draft

Hotseat

Two people, one keyboard, one browser tab. Tic-tac-toe with no accounts, no network, and no waiting — you pass the laptop, not a turn token.

Status Draft for review Scope ~1 week, one engineer Surface Single static page

The idea

Almost every tic-tac-toe implementation online solves the wrong problem: it builds an AI opponent, or it builds matchmaking. The actual thing people do with tic-tac-toe is play it with someone sitting next to them, on a napkin.

Hotseat is that napkin. One page, two players alternating on the same device. The entire product is a board, a turn indicator, and a reset button — and the work is almost entirely in making those three things feel good rather than in making them exist.

Scope

Goals

  • A full game playable in under ten seconds
  • Whose turn it is, unmistakable at a glance
  • Wins announced with the winning line shown, not just stated
  • Fully keyboard playable
  • Works offline; single file, no build step

Non-goals

  • Any computer opponent
  • Networked or remote play
  • Accounts, persistence, or match history
  • Larger boards or variant rules
  • Animation beyond simple state transitions

Core loop

  1. Page loads with an empty board. X moves first — always, no coin flip.
  2. The active player clicks or keys into an empty square.
  3. The mark is placed; the square becomes permanently inert.
  4. Win and draw checks run against the new board.
  5. If the game is still live, the turn indicator flips and focus stays on the board.
  6. On a win, the three winning squares highlight and the board locks.
  7. Reset returns to an empty board. The loser of the previous game opens the next one.

Game state

The whole game is four fields. Everything else on screen is derived from them, which means there is exactly one thing to get right and nothing to keep in sync.

FieldTypeNotes
squares ("X"|"O"|null)[9] Flat, not nested. Indices 0–8 read left-to-right, top-to-bottom. Nesting buys nothing and complicates every check.
turn "X" | "O" Stored rather than derived from move count — it survives a future undo feature without rework.
result null | {winner, line}
| "draw"		 One field covering all three outcomes, so "is the game over" is a single null check.
opener "X" | "O" Who starts the next game. Alternates so the same player does not always get the first-move advantage.

Win detection

Eight lines, hardcoded as index triples. There is no clever generalisation worth writing here — the board will never be a different size, and a literal table is the easiest thing to read and the hardest thing to get wrong.

A draw is not its own check — it is simply nine filled squares with no winning line. Checking for it separately is how implementations end up declaring a draw on a winning final move.

Accessibility

A 3×3 grid is one of the few places where keyboard support is genuinely cheap and the payoff is immediate — it also happens to make the game playable without a mouse, which matters when two people are sharing one laptop.

  • The board is a role="grid"; arrow keys move between squares, wrapping at the edges.
  • Enter or Space places a mark. Occupied squares are focusable but announce as taken.
  • Turn changes and results are announced through a polite live region, so a screen reader user is not left guessing.
  • Marks are distinguished by glyph and colour, never colour alone.
  • Focus stays on the board after a move — passing a laptop is bad enough without also losing your place.

Milestones

Day 1

Playable

State model, click-to-place, win and draw detection, reset. Deliberately unstyled — the goal is a correct game, not a nice one.

Day 2–3

Legible

Board and mark design, turn indicator, winning-line highlight, result messaging. This is where most of the value lands.

Day 4

Keyboard and screen reader

Grid navigation, focus management, live-region announcements, contrast pass.

Day 5

Polish

Alternating openers, transitions, reduced-motion handling, and a pass on how it behaves on a phone held between two people.

Open questions

These are the decisions worth arguing about before any code exists. Everything else is legible from the plan above.

Do we keep a running score across games?

A best-of-five tally is the single most requested feature in every version of this that has ever existed, and it costs roughly one more state field. It also turns a ten-second toy into a session, which may or may not be what we want.

Leaning: yes, in-memory only, cleared on refresh.

Player names, or just X and O?

Names make the turn indicator far warmer — "Sam's turn" beats "X's turn". They also add a naming step before the first game, which is exactly the friction this product exists to avoid.

Leaning: X and O for v1, with names as an obvious follow-up.

Is there an undo?

Two people sharing a device misclick constantly. But undo in a two-player game is a social negotiation, not a button — and it invites exactly the argument the button was meant to settle.

Leaning: no undo; make the squares large enough that misclicks are rare.

Who opens the next game?

Always-X is simplest but hands a measurable advantage to one seat. Alternating is fairer. Loser-opens is fairest but needs explaining, and the plan above quietly assumes it.

Leaning: alternate, and say so in the UI.

Risks

Scope creep into an AI opponent
It is the most tempting addition and it changes the product entirely. Listed as a non-goal specifically so the conversation is short.
Over-animating
A ten-second game cannot afford a one-second win animation. Cap transitions well under 200ms and honour reduced-motion.
Draws feeling anticlimactic
Most competent games end in a draw. If the draw state is a shrug, the product feels broken rather than correct.
Phone layout as an afterthought
Two people sharing a phone is a likely real use, and a 76px board does not survive it unexamined.
